\section{Main Conclusions}

Neural networks are successful in capturing, with a relatively small number of neurons, the behaviour of complex models with a large number of degrees of freedom, such as the Heston model. In this work, we developed and evaluated neural-network-based surrogates for calibration, pricing, and Greek computation of put derivatives. Achieving high accuracy and computational efficiency required several technical and benchmarking choices.

The key idea behind the success of our approach is the use of a dedicated neural network architecture for each task. By reusing a common framework while adapting each network to its target problem, we obtain robust and efficient approximations. This is also the main interpretation of the results in Chapter~\ref{ch:performance_benchmarks}: the ANN, CaNN, PINN, Sobolev, and ACV components should not be read as a single sequence of interchangeable models, but as task-specific tools evaluated under the same numerical benchmark.

Computing an implied volatility surface typically requires the iterative evaluation of an option pricer within an optimization algorithm. The supervised ANN surrogate introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:ann_surrogate} condenses this process into a single evaluation of the network, producing implied volatilities computed with errors in the \(10^{-3}\) IV range on held-out data while maintaining very fast inference times, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ann_global_metrics}. Figure~\ref{fig:extrapolation_grid_ann} shows that this surrogate is most reliable inside the interpolation region. This makes the ANN branch the natural starting point for the calibration part of the thesis, because the inverse problem needs many repeated evaluations of the forward implied-volatility map.

The calibration of the Heston model is similarly challenging, as state-of-the-art approaches generally rely on carefully designed optimization loops and derivative information \cite{cui2017full_fast_heston}. By leveraging the CaNN methodology introduced by Liu et al.~\cite{liu}, we use the previously trained ANN surrogate to recover the Heston model parameters from a given implied volatility surface. On the replicated \(35\)-quote Liu grid, the CaNN calibration reduces the IV MSE to the \(10^{-10}\) range with the baseline ANN and to the \(10^{-11}\) range with the Sobolev ANN, compared with the \(10^{-8}\) reference scale reported by Liu et al. More importantly, all reported Heston parameter errors improve relative to the Liu et al. reference values under the same synthetic-truth comparison reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:performance_benchmarks}. In the final Sobolev CaNN benchmark, the MSE improves by more than two orders of magnitude, while the parameter errors improve from roughly half an order of magnitude to more than two orders of magnitude depending on the parameter; the detailed comparison is reported in Table~\ref{tab:cann_vs_liu}.

Taking the proposed model from Liu one step forward, we improved the ANN performance by adding information about the Greeks via Sobolev augmentation. This improves both the RMSE of pricing and Greek computation. In the ANN branch, the largest effect is observed in the sensitivity diagnostics: Sobolev training reduces the Greek RMSE for all reported quantities, with especially clear gains in Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Rho, as reported in Table~\ref{tab:ann_sobolev_greek_rmse}. The distributional and spatial diagnostics in Figures~\ref{fig:ann_baseline_sobolev_greek_error_histograms} and~\ref{fig:ann_baseline_sobolev_greek_rel_error_maps} show that this improvement is broad, although not uniform over the whole \((m,\tau)\) domain. Therefore, the Sobolev ANN should not be interpreted only as a more accurate pointwise regressor, but as a derivative-aware surrogate whose learned surface is more suitable for calibration diagnostics and risk sensitivities.

We also evaluated the performance of PINNs regarding pricing and Greek computation. While ANNs require previous knowledge about a large set of prices, implied volatilities, or Greeks, the baseline PINN relies on the Heston PDE, together with terminal and boundary conditions, to infer the price surface without supervised price labels. For this reason, the PINN is not better than the ANN branch in the global comparison of Table~\ref{tab:pinn_global_rmse_mape}, but it answers a different and important question: whether a useful pricing surface can be learned from the structure of the differential equation itself. The results show that this is possible, with fast repeated price evaluation and small pricing errors on the benchmark grid, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pinn_pricing_composite}. When dealing with Greek computation, however, the baseline PINN is more fragile, especially near short maturities. The combined action of Sobolev augmentation and the analytical control variate improves this behaviour: Sobolev terms reduce the full-grid Greek errors reported in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_metrics_by_greek}, while the ACV correction targets the short-maturity at-the-money region where the remaining derivative errors are most concentrated, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pinn_sobolev_acv_kink_rel_error_maps} and Table~\ref{tab:pinn_sobolev_acv_kink_rmse_mape}.

The main contribution of the thesis is therefore the construction of a reproducible benchmark across the main neural techniques considered for Heston-based pricing, calibration, and Greek computation. The comparison covers supervised ANN surrogates, CaNN calibration, Sobolev-augmented ANN models, baseline PINNs, Sobolev PINNs, and the Sobolev+ACV correction. More importantly, the benchmark does not rely only on aggregate errors: it reports IV and pricing metrics, Greek RMSEs, spatial diagnostics, hard-region behaviour, and evaluation throughput. The latter is relevant because the advantage of these models appears after training, when repeated evaluations can replace much slower numerical calls; this is visible in the ANN evaluator and in the PINN pricing benchmark, where the learned models reach orders-of-magnitude faster repeated evaluation than the corresponding reference procedures.

Taken together, these results support a practical division of roles. The ANN/CaNN block is the most mature part of the workflow and gives the strongest calibration and Greek-estimation results in the controlled benchmark. The PINN block is valuable as a differentiable pricing engine trained from the Heston PDE without supervised price labels, but its derivative quality requires additional structure. In our experiments, this structure appears either through Sobolev augmentation, which improves the global Greek diagnostics, or through analytical control variates tailored to the short-maturity at-the-money region.

\section{Future Work}

The most immediate extension would be to exploit put-call parity. Since the thesis has focused on European put options, the corresponding call prices can be obtained consistently from the same learned put surface under the usual parity relation. This would enlarge the set of vanilla instruments covered by the framework without changing the core Heston benchmark, and would also provide a first consistency check before moving to more complex payoffs.

From there, it would be natural to study more explicitly the interaction between networks that learn volatility surfaces and networks that produce prices and Greeks. In the present thesis these components have been benchmarked separately to keep the comparison clean. A more coupled approach could use calibrated implied-volatility surfaces as inputs for pricing and Greek engines, while derivative-aware models could provide diagnostics on the smoothness and stability of the learned volatility representation.

The controlled benchmark should also be extended to market data with arbitrage-aware implied-volatility surfaces. The synthetic Heston benchmark is useful because the true parameters and reference values are known, but real surfaces introduce noise, liquidity effects, bid-ask spreads, and static-arbitrage constraints. Extending the workflow to this setting would show whether the calibration and Greek improvements remain stable under realistic data imperfections.

A particularly important open question is how to improve PINN Greeks without introducing precomputed Greek targets in the loss. Sobolev augmentation improves the sensitivity diagnostics, but it also changes the information regime because derivative labels are added during training. A more self-contained PINN method should preserve the PDE-based character of the baseline model while improving derivative stability through other mechanisms, such as better loss balancing, adaptive sampling near difficult regions, architectures with smoother second derivatives, or analytical corrections that do not require supervised Greek data.

Beyond the vanilla setting, the same methodology could be tested on American, Bermudan, or Asian options. These products introduce early-exercise features or path dependence, so the validation setup would have to change. The neural architecture may still be reusable, but the reference engine and the training data would become more complex. This is precisely where neural surrogates may become more useful, because the cost of repeated numerical pricing grows quickly for these products.

These extensions would require a more complex setup, but they are realistic given recent progress in scientific machine-learning libraries, automatic differentiation, and neural PDE solvers. The benchmark built in this thesis provides a controlled baseline from which those more complex experiments can be added without losing diagnostic discipline.
