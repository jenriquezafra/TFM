\section{Motivation}
Financial derivatives are contracts whose payoff depends on the future value of another market variable, usually the price of an asset. Options are one of the most common examples: they give the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying asset at a fixed strike price \(K\), with the contract ending at a specified maturity date. In this thesis we focus on European put options. A put option pays when the underlying asset finishes below \(K\), and the European condition means that this payoff is evaluated only at maturity. This contract is simple enough to study under controlled assumptions, but it already contains the main numerical difficulties that appear in derivative pricing.

The natural language for this problem is stochastic calculus. Asset prices are not treated as deterministic curves, but as stochastic processes whose evolution contains a random component, usually driven by Brownian motion. Under this formulation, an option price is obtained from the expected discounted payoff under a pricing measure. Volatility measures the intensity of the random fluctuations of the asset, so it directly affects the distribution of future prices and, as a consequence, the value of the option. This is the mathematical setting behind both the classical Black-Scholes model and the Heston stochastic-volatility model used in this work.

In practice, option pricing is not only a question of computing one price. The same pricing model is often used many times: to generate prices for different strikes and maturities, to fit model parameters to quoted option prices, and to measure how sensitive the price is to changes in the inputs. These sensitivities are usually called Greeks. They are important because a small pricing error can become more relevant when the model is used inside calibration or risk-management routines.

A classical reference point is the Black-Scholes model \cite{black_scholes_1973}. It gives an explicit formula for European option prices in a simplified setting where volatility is assumed to be fixed. The model is still central because it provides a common reference for comparing option prices across strikes and maturities. In later chapters, this idea will also be connected to the market convention of expressing option prices through an equivalent constant volatility level, known as implied volatility. The limitation of Black-Scholes is that a single fixed volatility is usually not flexible enough to represent option prices across different strikes and maturities.

This thesis uses the Heston model \cite{heston} because it relaxes this constant-volatility assumption while keeping the problem numerically tractable. In Heston, the variance of the asset is itself modelled as a random process. This allows the model to represent richer price patterns than Black-Scholes, especially when prices vary across strikes and maturities. The price paid for this flexibility is that calibration becomes a harder inverse problem: instead of inferring a single volatility parameter, one must recover several parameters that control the variance process and its interaction with the asset price. At the same time, Heston keeps enough mathematical structure to be priced efficiently by the Fourier-COS method introduced in Chapter \ref{ch:background}. This makes it a suitable middle ground for the thesis: more expressive than the constant-volatility model, but still controlled enough to provide accurate reference prices.

Another standard way to price derivatives is Monte Carlo simulation \cite{boyle1977montecarlo}. The idea is to simulate many possible future paths of the underlying stochastic process and average the resulting discounted payoffs.
This approach is very flexible \todos{and computationally complex; MonteCarlo methods and stochastichs process , p.365 sobre el error, sect. 6.3.6.3} because it works directly with simulated paths and can be adapted to many payoffs and model dynamics. However, this flexibility comes with computational cost. The estimator contains sampling error, and reducing that error typically requires a large number of simulated paths.

Closed-form or semi-closed pricing formulas are available only in specific cases, such as European vanilla options under Black-Scholes or transform-based Heston pricing. For many other contracts, simulation or numerical solution methods are needed. Even so, Monte Carlo is not the main reference method in this thesis. The experiments require repeated pricing calls, implied-volatility generation, calibration tests, and Greek benchmarks. In that setting, Monte Carlo sampling noise would make the comparison with neural methods less clean. A deterministic Heston-COS reference is more appropriate for the benchmark developed here.

This repeated-use setting is where neural approximations become attractive. A neural network can be trained to approximate a numerical pricing routine and then evaluated very quickly.
This idea has been used in financial model calibration, where the expensive pricing map is replaced by a fast surrogate after training \cite{liu}. However, speed alone is not sufficient in a financial workflow. A learned component is useful only if its errors can be measured, if its weak regions can be detected, and if its outputs remain reliable when used in calibration or sensitivity analysis.

\section{Problem Setting}
This thesis focuses on European vanilla put options under the Heston stochastic-volatility model. A put option gives value to its holder when the underlying asset finishes below the strike price. In the European case considered here, the option is evaluated only at the maturity date. This setting is deliberately restricted: the payoff rule is simple, the exercise date is fixed, and the numerical reference can be built under controlled assumptions.
At the same time, the model is expressive enough to generate non-trivial behaviour across strikes, maturities, and volatility regimes.

The problem is therefore not the complexity of the contract itself, but the reliability of the computational workflow built around it. 
The thesis studies three connected tasks: approximating the outputs of the Heston pricing routine, using those approximations inside calibration, and evaluating the quality of the resulting sensitivities. Each of these tasks can fail in a different way.
A neural surrogate may have a small average pricing error but still introduce structured errors across moneyness or maturity. A calibration routine may fit observed quotes while recovering unstable parameter values. A differentiable pricing model may produce reasonable prices while giving inaccurate Greeks.

This is especially relevant in regions where the pricing surface changes rapidly. 
The most delicate cases in this thesis are short maturities and near-the-strike contracts, where the current underlying price is close to the strike. 
This region is often called at the money, but the important point here is that small movements in the underlying can produce large changes in local curvature. 
For a European put, the payoff changes slope abruptly at the strike: below \(K\) it increases as the asset price falls, while above \(K\) it is zero. This sharp corner is often called a payoff kink. Near maturity, it makes the pricing surface harder to approximate, especially for second-order sensitivities such as Gamma.

For this reason, the thesis treats benchmarking as part of the problem itself. Neural components are not evaluated only by speed or by global error metrics, but also by their behaviour in the downstream tasks for which they are intended. The central question is wheter these components can be useful inside a Heston pricing workflow without hiding errors that later affect calibration or Greek computation.


\section{Methodological Approach and Contributions}
The thesis is built around a benchmarked Heston workflow that combines a numerical reference layer with neural approximation components \todos{quitar lo de layers y que distinga entre SOTA ML related data generator; pero así queda raro} . 
The reference layer provides accurate option values and sensitivity benchmarks, which are used throughout the experiments as controlled comparison objects. 
This makes it possible to evaluate neural methods under explicit numerical conditions and to distinguish computational acceleration from genuine loss of reliability.

On top of this reference layer, the thesis studies two neural network approaches. 
The first is a supervised artificial neural network (ANN) surrogate trained to approximate the outputs of the Heston pricing routine. 
This surrogate is then used inside a calibration routine, where the objective is to recover model parameters efficiently from option quotes. 
The second approach is a physics-informed neural network (PINN) used to approximate the option price surface directly from the Heston pricing equation. 
Since this price surface is differentiable, it can also be used to compute Greeks though automatic differentiation \cite{baydin2018autodiff}.

A particular focus is placed on the quality of these Greeks.
The thesis does not treat sensitivity computation as a secondary output of the pricing model, but as a separate diagnostic problem. 
For this reason, the final part of the workflow studies two complementary ways of improving or analysing Greek accuarcy. 
One introduces derivative information during training, while the other uses an analytic reference component to correct or diagnose the learned sensitivities. 
These two ideas are later developed as the Sobolev-enhanced and analytic-control-variate experiments. 

The main contribution of the thesis is the construction and evaluation of this full workflow under a common benchmark.
The ANN calibration block is used to study whether neural surrogates can accelerate repeated Heston evaluations and parameter recovery. 
The PINN block is used to study wheter a neural price surface can provide reliable prices and sensitivities at the same time. 
The final experiments analyse the regions where Greek estimation is most difficult and assess wheter the two Greek-improvement strategies reduce those errors.


This organization is important for the interpretation of the results. 
The ANN is judged not only as a fast function approximator, but also as the forward engine used by the calibration routine. 
The PINN is judged not only by price error, but also by spatial error structure and derivative quality. 
The Greek analysis is treated as a separate problem because automatic differentiation through a learned price surface does not by itself guarantee reliable sensitivities.

\section{State of the Art}

The literature relevant to this thesis sits at the intersection of numerical option pricing, model calibration, and neural approximation. Classical model-based pricing provides the financial foundation. The Black--Scholes model gives a closed-form benchmark under constant volatility \cite{black_scholes_1973}, while the Heston model extends this framework by introducing stochastic variance and semi-analytical pricing formulas \cite{heston}. Simulation-based approaches, especially Monte Carlo methods, are also a central reference in derivative pricing because of their flexibility across payoffs and dynamics \cite{boyle1977montecarlo}. Their main drawback in the present setting is that repeated simulation can be expensive and introduces sampling noise, which is inconvenient when the objective is to build deterministic benchmarks for calibration and Greek diagnostics.

Fourier and spectral methods provide another important line of work. When the characteristic function of the model is available, as in Heston, transform methods can price options accurately without simulating paths. FFT-based pricing \cite{carr_madan_1999} and the Fourier-COS method \cite{fang_oosterlee_2009} are standard examples of this approach. The COS method is especially useful here because it gives a deterministic and accurate reference layer for repeated pricing, implied-volatility generation, and benchmark construction. Its performance, however, still depends on numerical choices such as the truncation interval, the number of expansion terms, and the stability of the implied volatility inversion.

Neural methods enter this landscape as acceleration and approximation tools. Feed-forward ANN surrogates can learn pricing or implied-volatility maps from synthetic labels and then evaluate them much faster than the numerical solver, which makes them attractive for calibration workflows \cite{liu}. Physics-informed neural networks follow a different route: instead of learning only from labels, they use the pricing equation itself as part of the training objective. Recent work applies this idea to Heston-type option pricing and related PDE settings \cite{hainaut2024pinn,yu2022gpinn}. At the same time, the PINN literature reports well-known difficulties with optimization, loss balancing, gradient flow, and robustness across the training domain \cite{wang2021gradientpath,wang2022pinnntk,urban_2025}.

For Greek computation, automatic differentiation provides a natural way to obtain sensitivities from differentiable neural price surfaces \cite{baydin2018autodiff}, although accurate prices do not automatically imply accurate derivatives. This motivates derivative-aware training strategies such as Sobolev-type supervision \cite{czarnecki2017sobolev} and correction or diagnostic tools based on analytic control variates, which are related to the broader variance-reduction tradition in Monte Carlo methods \cite{glasserman2004montecarlo}.

\section{Scope and Thesis Structure}

The scope is intentionally controlled. 
All experiments are carried out for European vanilla options under the Heston model, using synthetic data and numerical references whose settings can be inspected.
This restriction is appropriate for the objective of the work: to test whether neural components can accelerate pricing, calibration, and sensitivity computation while remaining comparable to established numerical methods.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces the mathematical and financial background needed for the rest of the work, including option pricing, the Heston model, Greeks, and the numerical methods used as referece.
Chapter~\ref{ch:ann_surrogate} develops the supervised neural methods for Heston pricing and calibration: the ANN surrogate pricer and the CaNN inverse workflow.
Chapter~\ref{ch:pinn_greeks} introduces the PINN framework for Heston pricing and sensitivity computation, including the extensions used to study Greeks accuracy.
Chapter~\ref{ch:performance_benchmarks} reports the numerical benchmarks for pricing, calibration, PINN performance, and Greek diagnostics.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarizes the main conclusions and discusses possible extensions.
