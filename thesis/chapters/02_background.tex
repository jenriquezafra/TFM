\chapter{Mathematical and Financial Background}
\label{ch:background}

\section{Mathematical Framework For Option Pricing}
This section fixes the common mathematical setup used in the rest of the thesis for European put pricing.

Following standard stochastic-process notation \cite[Ch.~1]{oksendal2003}, we work on a filtered probability space
\[
(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t)_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb P).
\]
Here, $\Omega$ is the set of possible outcomes, $\mathcal F$ is the collection of measurable events, $\mathbb P$ is the original probability measure, and $\mathcal F_t$ represents the information available up to time $t$. The filtration is increasing: if $s\leq t$, then $\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_t$, so information is accumulated over time.

The maturity is finite, $T>0$.
Time is continuous, $t\in[0,T]$, and the time to maturity is $\tau=T-t\in[0,T]$.
The underlying asset price $S=(S_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ is assumed to be positive and adapted to the filtration, meaning that $S_t$ is observable with the information available at time $t$.
The strike $K>0$ and the risk-free rate $r\geq0$ are constant.
When a fixed valuation date is needed, it is denoted by $t_0\in[0,T)$ and $S_0:=S_{t_0}$.
When it is useful to distinguish the two uses of moneyness, we write $m_0:=S_0/K$ for the fixed-date moneyness used in quote grids and $m:=S/K$ for the generic moneyness state used in PDE formulations. Thus, $m_0$ denotes the coordinate observed at the valuation date, while $m$ denotes the state variable over which the pricing equation is written.

The contract studied throughout the numerical pipeline is a European vanilla put.
Its terminal payoff is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:european_put_payoff}
    \Psi_{\mathrm{put}}=(K-S_T)^+,\qquad (x)^+=\max(x,0).
\end{equation}
The payoff changes slope at the strike, $S_T=K$. This non-differentiable point is often referred to as the payoff kink.
Figure~\ref{fig:put_payoff} shows the normalized put payoff $\Psi_{\mathrm{put}}/K$ as a function of the normalized terminal price $S_T/K$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/put_payoff_matplotlib.pdf}
    \caption{Normalized terminal payoff of a European put. The holder has the right, but not the obligation, to exercise at maturity; therefore the payoff is positive only when $S_T/K<1$ and is zero for $S_T/K\geq 1$.}
    \label{fig:put_payoff}
\end{figure}

Although the numerical pipeline in this thesis is built around European puts, the corresponding relation with European calls will also be useful. In the no-dividend setting, European call and put prices with the same underlying, strike, maturity, and risk-free rate satisfy the put-call parity relation \cite{hull_options}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:put_call_parity}
    V_t^{\mathrm{call}}-V_t^{\mathrm{put}}
    =
    S_t-Ke^{-r(T-t)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in terms of time to maturity $\tau=T-t$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:put_from_call_parity}
    V_t^{\mathrm{put}}
    =
    V_t^{\mathrm{call}}-S_t+Ke^{-r\tau}.
\end{equation}
This no-arbitrage identity will be used later to translate call-based Heston formulas into put quantities and to define parity residuals in the numerical benchmark.

A global notation glossary is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:global_glossary} and will be extended as new symbols are introduced.

For valuation, assume that there exists an equivalent risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q$ under which discounted tradable prices are martingales. The standard risk-neutral valuation formula for the European payoffs considered in this thesis is \cite[Ch.~8]{oksendal2003}
\begin{equation}
    V_t=e^{-r(T-t)}\,\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\!\left[\Psi\mid\mathcal F_t\right]
    =e^{-r\tau}\,\mathbb E_t^{\mathbb Q}[\Psi],
    \qquad 0\leq t\leq T.
\end{equation}
Here, $V_t$ is the option value at time $t$, $\mathbb E_t^{\mathbb Q}[\cdot]$ denotes the conditional expectation given $\mathcal F_t$, and in the put case $\Psi=\Psi_{\mathrm{put}}$. 
The risk-neutral measure is the pricing probability measure under which discounted tradable asset prices have no expected excess return. 
This is why future payoffs can be evaluated by taking conditional expectations under $\mathbb Q$ and discounting at the risk-free rate $r$.

In the following sections, this framework is connected to the main ingredients used throughout the thesis: the probability notation behind transform pricing, the stochastic-calculus tools needed for the model equations, the Black--Scholes benchmark, the Heston stochastic-volatility dynamics, and the characteristic-function/Fourier-COS pricing route.

\section{Probability Theory}
\label{sec:probability_theory_background}
This section collects the probability notation used later in the transform-pricing formulas. We introduce distribution functions, densities, characteristic functions, and cumulants, since these objects are needed to construct the Fourier-COS pricing representation. The presentation follows standard probability definitions and the transform-pricing notation adopted in \cite[Ch.~1]{oksendal2003} and \cite[Sec.~2]{fang_oosterlee_2009}.

For a real random variable $X$, the cumulative distribution function is
\begin{equation}
    F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\le x).
\end{equation}
Here, $x$ is a real evaluation point and $\mathbb P$ denotes probability.
If $F_X$ is differentiable, the associated probability density function is
\begin{equation}
    f_X(x)=\frac{\partial F_X(x)}{\partial x}.
\end{equation}

Following this notation, the characteristic function of $X$ is
\begin{equation}
    \phi_X(u) :=\mathbb E\left[ e^{iuX} \right] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{iux}dF_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{iux}f_X(x)dx,
\end{equation}
\noindent for $u\in\mathbb R$, where $i$ is the imaginary unit.

Applying the logarithm to $\phi_X$, we obtain the cumulant characteristic function
\begin{equation}
    \zeta_X(u) = \log \mathbb E\left[e^{iuX}\right] = \log \phi_X(u).
\end{equation}
Its Maclaurin expansion is
\begin{equation}
    \zeta_X(u) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\partial^k \zeta_X (u)}{\partial u^k}\bigg| _{u=0}\frac{u^k}{k!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\zeta_k \frac{u^k}{k!},
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_k \equiv\frac{\partial^k \zeta_X(u)}{\partial u^k}\bigg|_{u=0}$ is the $k$-th cumulant.


The moment generating function is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal M_X(u):= \phi_X(-iu) = \mathbb E\left[ e^{uX} \right]. 
\end{equation}


Using $\mathcal M_X(u)=\phi_X(-iu)$ and $\zeta_X(u)=\log \mathcal M_X(u)$, cumulants can be computed from the characteristic function as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cumulants}
    \zeta_k =\frac{\partial ^k \zeta_X(u)}{\partial u^k}\bigg|_{u=0}=\frac{\partial ^k}{\partial u^k}\log \phi_X(-iu)\bigg|_{u=0}.
\end{equation}
These objects are central in the rest of this chapter: the characteristic function provides direct access to Fourier-domain pricing formulas, and low-order cumulants are later used to construct truncation intervals for the COS method.

\section{Stochastic Calculus Background}
\label{sec:stochastic_calculus_background}
The Black--Scholes and Heston dynamics introduced below are written as stochastic differential equations (SDEs), so they require a continuous-time source of randomness. Following standard SDE notation \cite[Chs.~2--4]{oksendal2003}, a Brownian motion, also called a Wiener process, is an adapted process $W_t$ with $W_0=0$, continuous paths, independent increments, and $W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,t-s)$ for $0\leq s<t\leq T$. In stochastic differential equations, $dW_t$ denotes the infinitesimal random component generated by this process. If two Brownian motions are correlated, their instantaneous quadratic covariation is written as $d\langle W^{(1)},W^{(2)}\rangle_t=\rho\,dt$, with $\rho\in[-1,1]$.

More generally, the asset dynamics used in this thesis are examples of It\^o diffusions. In one dimension, such a process can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ito_diffusion}
    dX_t=\mu(t,X_t)\,dt+\eta(t,X_t)\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the drift coefficient and $\eta$ is the diffusion coefficient. The drift describes the deterministic infinitesimal trend, while the diffusion coefficient scales the random Brownian shock. Black--Scholes corresponds to a one-dimensional diffusion with proportional volatility, whereas Heston extends the state by adding a second stochastic equation for the variance.

The main calculus rule for such processes is It\^o's formula. If $f(t,x)$ is sufficiently smooth and $X_t$ satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq:ito_diffusion}, then
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ito_formula}
    df(t,X_t)
    =
    \left(
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
    +\mu\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
    +\frac{1}{2}\eta^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}
    \right)dt
    +\eta\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dW_t.
\end{equation}
The second-order term is the distinctive feature of It\^o calculus and comes from the quadratic variation of Brownian motion. In multidimensional models such as Heston, the same principle applies with cross-derivative terms determined by the instantaneous covariations between the Brownian drivers.

This connection is also what links the stochastic dynamics to pricing PDEs. Under the risk-neutral measure, if $V(t,x)$ denotes the price of a European claim with terminal payoff $\Psi(X_T)$, the Feynman--Kac representation relates the discounted conditional expectation to a partial differential equation of the form
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:feynman_kac_generic}
    \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
    +\mathcal{L}V
    -rV
    =0,
    \qquad
    V(T,x)=\Psi(x),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the infinitesimal generator of the diffusion. In Chapter~\ref{ch:pinn_greeks}, this link is used in the reverse direction: starting from the Heston SDEs, the PINN branch enforces the associated pricing PDE instead of learning from simulated paths or external price labels.

\section{The Black--Scholes Model and Implied Volatility}
\label{sec:black_scholes_model}

To understand the Heston model, it is necessary to first recall the Black--Scholes model \cite{black_scholes_1973,hull_options}, because Heston can be read as a direct extension of its constant-volatility dynamics. In Black--Scholes, volatility is a fixed parameter: all randomness in the underlying price comes from a single Brownian driver with constant diffusion scale. This gives a closed-form pricing formula and, more importantly for this thesis, provides the convention used to define implied volatility. In the no-dividend Black--Scholes model, under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q$, the underlying follows the geometric Brownian motion
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bs_dynamics}
    dS_t = rS_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb Q},
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the constant risk-free rate, $W_t^{\mathbb Q}$ is a Brownian motion under $\mathbb Q$, and $\sigma>0$ is the constant volatility parameter. This is the modelling restriction that Heston relaxes by replacing the fixed variance level with a stochastic variance process.
In the notation of Equation~\eqref{eq:ito_diffusion}, this is a one-dimensional It\^o diffusion with drift $\mu(t,S_t)=rS_t$ and diffusion coefficient $\eta(t,S_t)=\sigma S_t$.

For a European put, the corresponding Black--Scholes price is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bs_put_formula}
    V_{BS}^{\mathrm{put}}(S_0,K,r,\tau,\sigma)
    =Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_2)-S_0\Phi(-d_1),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bs_d1_d2}
    d_1=\frac{\log(S_0/K)+(r+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},
    \qquad
    d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{\tau}.
\end{equation}
Given a target European put value $V_{\text{target}}$, its implied volatility is the value $\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}$ that reproduces this price when inserted into the same Black--Scholes formula:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bs_iv_definition}
    V_{BS}^{\mathrm{put}}(S_0,K,r,\tau,\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}})=V_{\text{target}}.
\end{equation}

Thus, implied volatility is a transformation of prices into volatility units; it is not a structural parameter of the Heston model. It is nevertheless the main comparison space in this thesis, because option quotes and calibration errors are usually interpreted across moneyness and maturity in implied-volatility units. The numerical inversion used to compute $\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}$ is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:iv_numerical_inversion}.

\section{The Heston Stochastic Volatility Model}
\label{sec:heston_model}

The Heston model \cite{heston} extends the constant-volatility benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:black_scholes_model} by making variance stochastic. In the risk-neutral pricing form used in this thesis, the dynamics are given by the following system of coupled stochastic differential equations:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:heston_dynamics}
    \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
    &dS_t = rS_t\,dt+\sqrt{\nu_t}\,S_t\,dW_t^{(S,\mathbb Q)},\\
    &d\nu_t = \kappa(\bar\nu-\nu_t)\,dt+\gamma\sqrt{\nu_t}\,dW_t^{(\nu,\mathbb Q)},\\
    &d\langle W^{(S,\mathbb Q)},W^{(\nu,\mathbb Q)}\rangle_t = \rho\,dt.
    \end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation}
Here, $S_t$ is the underlying price, $\nu_t$ is the instantaneous variance, and $W_t^{(S,\mathbb Q)}$ and $W_t^{(\nu,\mathbb Q)}$ are Brownian motions under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q$. In this background chapter we write the variance process as $\nu_t$. Later, in the implementation-oriented chapters, the same variance state is denoted by $v_t$; in particular, the initial variance at the valuation date is $\nu_0=\nu_{t_0}$ here and $v_0$ in the dataset notation. The variance equation is a square-root, or CIR-type, diffusion: it is mean-reverting toward the long-run level $\bar\nu$ at speed $\kappa$, and its random variation has instantaneous scale $\gamma\sqrt{\nu_t}$. Thus, $\gamma$ is the volatility of variance, often called vol-of-vol.
Compared with the one-dimensional diffusion in Equation~\eqref{eq:ito_diffusion}, Heston is a two-dimensional It\^o diffusion with correlated Brownian drivers; this correlation is what generates the mixed derivative term in the Heston pricing PDE used later in Chapter~\ref{ch:pinn_greeks}.

The model parameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:heston_parameters}. The table keeps the interpretation operational: each row states how the parameter enters the dynamics and which part of the generated price or IV grid it most directly affects.
\begin{table}[H]
    \ttabbox[\FBwidth]
    {\caption{Interpretation of Heston parameters}\label{tab:heston_parameters}}
    {\begin{tabular}{|P{2.2cm}|P{4.3cm}|P{5.3cm}|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Role in the dynamics} & \textbf{Main practical effect} \\
        \hline
        $\rho$ & Instantaneous correlation between the Brownian drivers of price and variance. & Changes the asymmetry of prices and IV values across moneyness. \\
        \hline
        $\gamma$ & Volatility of the variance process, or vol-of-vol. & Controls the amplitude of random variation in the variance path. \\
        \hline
        $\bar\nu$ & Long-run mean level of the variance process. & Sets the level toward which variance reverts. \\
        \hline
        $\nu_0$ & Initial variance level at valuation time ($\nu_{t_0}$). & Sets the starting variance state, which is especially relevant for short maturities. \\
        \hline
        $\kappa$ & Speed of mean reversion of $\nu_t$ toward $\bar\nu$. & Controls how quickly variance moves back toward its long-run level. \\
        \hline
        \multicolumn{3}{l}{Source: Based on \cite{heston} and \cite[Chs.~2--3]{rouah_heston_book}}
    \end{tabular}}
\end{table}

A common positivity condition for the CIR variance process is the Feller condition
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:feller_condition}
    2\kappa\bar\nu \ge \gamma^2.
\end{equation}
In practice, calibrated parameter sets may violate this inequality while still producing usable market fits; therefore, it is treated as a diagnostic and stability indicator rather than as a hard calibration constraint in many numerical workflows \cite[Chs.~2--3]{rouah_heston_book}.

The other output quantities used later are Greeks. To keep them distinct from Heston parameters such as $\gamma$ and $\rho$, Greek names are written in roman text. For a value function $V(S,\nu,t;r)$, we use
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{Delta} &= \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}, &
    \mathrm{Gamma} &= \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}, \\[0.35em]
    \mathrm{Vega} &= \frac{\partial V}{\partial \nu}, &
    \mathrm{Rho} &= \frac{\partial V}{\partial r}, \\[0.35em]
    \mathrm{Theta} &= \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Delta is the first-order sensitivity to the underlying price. Gamma is the second-order sensitivity to the underlying price. In this thesis, Vega denotes sensitivity with respect to the Heston variance state; in constant-volatility models Vega is often defined with respect to volatility instead. Rho is the sensitivity to the risk-free rate, and Theta is the sensitivity to calendar time, equivalently the negative derivative with respect to time to maturity.

\section{Pricing Via Characteristic Functions}
\label{sec:pricing_characteristic_functions}
We now turn to the Fourier representation used for pricing. The relevant object is the characteristic function of a log-price state. For example, if $X_T=\log S_T$, then
\begin{equation}
    \phi_X(u;t,S_t,\nu_t)
    =\mathbb E_t^{\mathbb Q}\!\left[e^{iuX_T}\mid S_t,\nu_t\right].
\end{equation}
When pricing by log-moneyness, the same definition is applied to $Y_T=\log(S_T/K)$. Under Heston dynamics, this characteristic function has a semi-closed representation \cite{heston}, which is why the model is well suited to the Fourier-COS pricing route developed in the next sections.

This transform representation is useful because the density of the terminal log-state is usually not handled directly in pricing. Fourier pricing instead works with the characteristic function and recovers prices through transform integrals.

Using the density and characteristic-function notation introduced above, let $x=X(t_0)$ be the current state and $y=X(T)$ the terminal state. The discounted valuation formula can be written as
\begin{equation}
    V(t_0,x)=e^{-r\tau}\int_{\mathbb R}V(T,y)\,f_X(T,y;t_0,x)\,dy.
\end{equation}
Here, $V(T,y)$ is the terminal payoff as a function of the terminal state, and $f_X(T,y;t_0,x)$ is the density of $X(T)=y$ conditioned on the current state $X(t_0)=x$.
The corresponding characteristic function is
\begin{equation}
    \phi_X(u;t_0,x):=\mathbb E_{t_0}^{\mathbb Q}\!\left[e^{iuX(T)}\mid X(t_0)=x\right]
    =\int_{\mathbb R}e^{iuy}f_X(T,y;t_0,x)\,dy.
\end{equation}
If $f_X(T,\cdot;t_0,x)$ exists and $\int_{\mathbb R}|\phi_X(u;t_0,x)|\,du<\infty$, Fourier inversion gives:
\begin{equation}
    f_X(T,y;t_0,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-iuy}\phi_X(u;t_0,x)\,du.
\end{equation}
Let $\hat V_T(u):=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-iuy}V(T,y)\,dy$ denote the Fourier transform of the payoff at maturity. Substituting the inverse representation of $f_X$ into the pricing integral and exchanging the order of integration gives
\begin{equation}
    V(t_0,x)=e^{-r\tau}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\phi_X(u;t_0,x)\,\hat V_T(u)\,du.
\end{equation}

Compared with PDE solvers, this approach avoids full space-time gridding. Compared with Monte Carlo, it is deterministic and does not introduce sampling noise. In repeated-pricing tasks (surface generation, calibration loops, and large synthetic datasets), this is a major practical advantage.


The Fourier-COS method used in the next section is a specific spectral discretization of this transform-based pricing framework: it replaces the continuous integral by a finite cosine expansion over a truncated interval, preserving high accuracy with efficient vectorized evaluation.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{The Fourier-COS method}
\label{sec:fourier_cos_method}
The previous section showed that option values can be written as transform integrals once the characteristic function of the terminal state is known. The Fourier-COS method makes this representation numerically usable by replacing the terminal-state density on a finite interval with a cosine expansion \cite[Secs.~3--4]{fang_oosterlee_2009}. The key point is that the cosine coefficients of this density can be computed directly from the characteristic function, so pricing reduces to evaluating the characteristic function on a fixed frequency grid and summing finitely many terms.

Its practical appeal in this thesis is that it combines spectral convergence with efficient vectorized evaluation once the model characteristic function is available.

Let $x=X(t_0)$, $y=X(T)$, and let $[a,b]\subset\mathbb R$, with $a<b$, be a truncation interval chosen to concentrate most of the probability mass of $X(T)\mid X(t_0)=x$. On this interval, the density of the terminal state is approximated by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_density_expansion}
    \hat f_X(y)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\!'\ \bar F_k\cos\left(\pi k\frac{y-a}{b-a}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\hat f_X$ is the truncated cosine approximation of the density, $N\in\mathbb N$ is the number of cosine terms, $k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ indexes the expansion terms, $\sum'$ indicates that the $k=0$ term is weighted by $1/2$, and the cosine coefficients are obtained from the characteristic function:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_density_coefficients}
    \bar F_k:=\frac{2}{b-a}\Re\!\left[\phi_X\!\left(\frac{\pi k}{b-a}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{i\pi ak}{b-a}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
The operator $\Re[\cdot]$ denotes the real part; for example, $\Re[\alpha+i\beta]=\alpha$ for $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$.

Starting from
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_pricing_integral}
    V(t_0,x)=e^{-r\tau}\int_{\mathbb R}V(T,y)f_X(T,y;t_0,x)\,dy,
\end{equation}
the COS discretization yields
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_price_approximation}
    V(t_0,x)\approx e^{-r\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\!'\ \Re\!\left[\phi_X\!\left(\frac{\pi k}{b-a}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{i\pi ak}{b-a}\right)\right]H_k,
\end{equation}
where the payoff coefficients $H_k$, for $k=0,\ldots,N-1$, are
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_payoff_coefficients}
    H_k:=\frac{2}{b-a}\int_a^b V(T,y)\cos\left(\frac{y-a}{b-a}\pi k\right)\,dy.
\end{equation}
For plain-vanilla options, $H_k$ has closed-form expressions, so the numerical effort is concentrated in evaluating the characteristic function on a fixed frequency grid \cite[Sec.~3]{fang_oosterlee_2009}.

The choice of $[a,b]$ controls truncation error. Following the cumulant-based rule in \cite[Sec.~5]{fang_oosterlee_2009}, we use
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_cumulant_interval}
    [a,b]=\left[c_1-L\sqrt{c_2+\sqrt{c_4}},\;c_1+L\sqrt{c_2+\sqrt{c_4}}\right],
\end{equation}
with $c_1,c_2,c_4$ the first, second, and fourth cumulants of $X(T)$ and $L>0$ a scaling parameter. This connects directly with the cumulant machinery introduced in Section~\ref{sec:probability_theory_background}.
In the numerical experiments of this thesis, the integration interval is selected using the refined cumulant-based strategy detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:cos_error}.

The cumulant interval in Equation~\eqref{eq:cos_cumulant_interval} addresses the state-space truncation part of the approximation, but it does not by itself control the error introduced by using only $N$ cosine terms. It is therefore useful to separate two error sources:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cos_error_split}
    \left|V-V_N^{\mathrm{COS}}\right|
    \le
    \underbrace{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{space}}([a,b])}_{\text{state-space truncation}}
    +
    \underbrace{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{series}}(N;[a,b])}_{\text{finite COS series}}.
\end{equation}
Here, $V_N^{\mathrm{COS}}$ is the COS approximation using $N$ terms, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{space}}$ is the error from replacing the full state space $\mathbb R$ by the interval $[a,b]$, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{series}}$ is the error from keeping only finitely many cosine terms. Increasing $L$ reduces $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{space}}$ but may require larger $N$ to keep $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{series}}$ small; reducing $L$ has the opposite effect. In practice, we choose $(L,N)$ jointly and verify stability, especially for extreme strikes and short maturities, where numerical artifacts appear first.

Implementation is fully vectorized: frequencies $u_k=\pi k/(b-a)$, exponential phase factors, and payoff coefficients are precomputed once per maturity, then reused across strike grids. This is one of the reasons COS is well suited for repeated-pricing workflows such as calibration loops and synthetic dataset generation.

\subsection{The COS Method Applied To The Heston Model}
Following the notation of Sections~\ref{sec:pricing_characteristic_functions}--\ref{sec:fourier_cos_method} and \cite[Sec.~3]{fang_oosterlee_2009}, define the COS frequencies and log-moneyness variables as
\[
u_k:=\frac{k\pi}{b-a},\quad k=0,\ldots,N-1,\qquad x:=\log\!\left(\frac{S_0}{K}\right),\qquad y:=\log\!\left(\frac{S_T}{K}\right).
\]
Here, $x$ and $y$ are log-moneyness variables: they measure the underlying price relative to the strike before and at maturity. Positive log-moneyness means the underlying is above the strike, while negative log-moneyness means it is below the strike.
Under Heston dynamics, the characteristic function of $y$ can be written in semi-closed form \cite{heston,rouah_heston_book}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:heston_log_moneyness_cf}
    \varphi_H(u;\tau,x,\nu_0)=\exp\!\left(C(u,\tau)+D(u,\tau)\nu_0+iux\right),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    d(u)&=\sqrt{\left(\kappa-i\rho\gamma u\right)^2+\gamma^2\left(u^2+iu\right)},\\
    g(u)&=\frac{\kappa-i\rho\gamma u-d(u)}{\kappa-i\rho\gamma u+d(u)},\\
    C(u,\tau)&=iur\tau+\frac{\kappa\bar\nu}{\gamma^2}\!\left[\left(\kappa-i\rho\gamma u-d(u)\right)\tau-2\log\!\left(\frac{1-g(u)e^{-d(u)\tau}}{1-g(u)}\right)\right],\\
    D(u,\tau)&=\frac{\kappa-i\rho\gamma u-d(u)}{\gamma^2}\left(\frac{1-e^{-d(u)\tau}}{1-g(u)e^{-d(u)\tau}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\varphi_H$ denotes the Heston characteristic function of the log-moneyness state, while $C$, $D$, $d$, and $g$ are complex-valued auxiliary functions of the Fourier variable $u$ and maturity $\tau$. This is the standard semi-closed representation used in Fourier pricing; numerically, consistency in branch selection for $d(u)$ and the complex logarithm is required for stability \cite[Chs.~2--3]{rouah_heston_book}.

The COS price for one strike can then be written as
\begin{equation}
    V(t_0,x)\approx Ke^{-r\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\!'\ \Re\!\left[\varphi_H(u_k;\tau,x,\nu_0)\,e^{-iu_ka}\right]U_k,
\end{equation}
where $U_k$ are payoff-dependent COS coefficients for $k=0,\ldots,N-1$. For plain-vanilla options:
\begin{equation}
    U_k^{\mathrm{call}}=\frac{2}{b-a}\left(\chi_k(0,b)-\psi_k(0,b)\right),\qquad
    U_k^{\mathrm{put}}=\frac{2}{b-a}\left(-\chi_k(a,0)+\psi_k(a,0)\right),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \chi_k(\ell,q)
    &=\frac{1}{1+u_k^2}\Big[
        e^q\!\left(\cos(u_k(q-a))+u_k\sin(u_k(q-a))\right)\\
    &\hspace{3.2cm}
        -e^\ell\!\left(\cos(u_k(\ell-a))+u_k\sin(u_k(\ell-a))\right)
    \Big],\\
    \psi_k(\ell,q)
    &=
    \begin{cases}
        q-\ell, & k=0,\\[2mm]
        \dfrac{b-a}{k\pi}\left[\sin(u_k(q-a))-\sin(u_k(\ell-a))\right], & k\ge 1.
    \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\chi_k$ and $\psi_k$ are analytical payoff integrals over generic bounds $\ell$ and $q$ satisfying $a\le \ell<q\le b$.
In the implementation of this thesis, data generation is run on European puts as a numerical convention, mainly associated with the COS-pricing setup used in the project configuration.

This subsection establishes the analytical Heston-COS pricing block used as the reference framework in the following chapters.

\subsection{Semi-Analytical Greeks from the Heston Characteristic Function}
\label{sec:heston_cf_greeks}

For validation purposes, and later as derivative targets for Sobolev training and as references in the model comparisons, the option Greeks defined in Section~\ref{sec:heston_model} can also be computed directly from the Heston characteristic-function pricing representation. A standard presentation is often written for a European call \cite{heston} and is developed in \cite[Chapter~1]{rouah_heston_book}
\begin{equation}
V^{\mathrm{call}}(S_0,K,\tau)=S_0\,\Pi_1-Ke^{-r\tau}\,\Pi_2,
\end{equation}
where $V^{\mathrm{call}}(S_0,K,\tau)$ is the European call price and $\Pi_1,\Pi_2$ are risk-neutral probability-type terms in the Heston pricing formula:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_j=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty
\Re\!\left(
\frac{e^{-iu\log K}\,\Psi_j(u)}{iu}
\right)\,du,\qquad j\in\{1,2\},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\Psi_2(u)=\phi_X(u),\qquad
\Psi_1(u)=\frac{\phi_X(u-i)}{\phi_X(-i)},
\end{equation}
where \(j\) selects the two probability terms, \(\Psi_j\) are the corresponding Fourier integrands, and \(\phi_X\) is the characteristic function of \(X_T=\log S_T\) under Heston \cite{heston}; see also \cite[Chapter~1]{rouah_heston_book}. For puts, the same characteristic-function machinery is used through the put payoff coefficients or, equivalently, through the put-call parity relation in Equation~\eqref{eq:put_call_parity}.

Let \(p\) denote a generic model/input variable (\(r,\tau,\nu_0,\rho,\kappa,\gamma,\bar\nu\), etc.). If \(\partial_p\Psi_j(u)\) exists and the differentiated integrand is integrable on \((0,\infty)\), differentiation under the integral sign yields \cite[Chapter~11]{rouah_heston_book}
\begin{equation}
\partial_p\Pi_j=
\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty
\Re\!\left(
\frac{e^{-iu\log K}}{iu}\,\partial_p\Psi_j(u)
\right)\,du.
\end{equation}
Therefore, Greeks are obtained by combining the derivatives of \(\Pi_1,\Pi_2\) through the pricing identity \cite[Chapter~11]{rouah_heston_book}. For example:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Delta}_{\mathrm{call}} &=
\partial_{S_0}V^{\mathrm{call}}
=\Pi_1+S_0\,\partial_{S_0}\Pi_1-Ke^{-r\tau}\partial_{S_0}\Pi_2,\\
\mathrm{Gamma}_{\mathrm{call}} &=
\partial^2_{S_0}V^{\mathrm{call}}
=2\partial_{S_0}\Pi_1+S_0\partial^2_{S_0}\Pi_1
-Ke^{-r\tau}\partial^2_{S_0}\Pi_2,\\
\mathrm{Vega}_{\mathrm{call}} &=
\partial_{\nu_0}V^{\mathrm{call}}
=S_0\partial_{\nu_0}\Pi_1-Ke^{-r\tau}\partial_{\nu_0}\Pi_2,\\
\mathrm{Rho}_{\mathrm{call}} &=
\partial_r V^{\mathrm{call}}
=S_0\partial_r\Pi_1
+K\tau e^{-r\tau}\Pi_2
-Ke^{-r\tau}\partial_r\Pi_2,\\
\mathrm{Theta}_{\mathrm{call}} &=
\partial_t V^{\mathrm{call}}
=-\partial_\tau V^{\mathrm{call}}.
\end{align}

The thesis focuses on European puts, so the corresponding quantities are obtained by differentiating the put-call parity relation in Equation~\eqref{eq:put_from_call_parity}. This gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Delta}_{\mathrm{put}}&=\mathrm{Delta}_{\mathrm{call}}-1, &
\mathrm{Gamma}_{\mathrm{put}}&=\mathrm{Gamma}_{\mathrm{call}}, &
\mathrm{Vega}_{\mathrm{put}}&=\mathrm{Vega}_{\mathrm{call}},\\
\mathrm{Rho}_{\mathrm{put}}&=\mathrm{Rho}_{\mathrm{call}}-\tau K e^{-r\tau}, &
\mathrm{Theta}_{\mathrm{put}}&=\mathrm{Theta}_{\mathrm{call}}+rK e^{-r\tau}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The practical advantage is that all sensitivities are expressed in terms of derivatives of the same characteristic-function integrands. Equation~\eqref{eq:heston_log_moneyness_cf} shows that the Heston characteristic function is built from \(C(u,\tau)\), \(D(u,\tau)\nu_0\), and the log-state term. Therefore, parameter derivatives can be obtained by differentiating these components and inserting the resulting integrands into the formulas above.

\section{Implied Volatility And Numerical Inversion}
\label{sec:iv_numerical_inversion}
Implied volatility was defined in Section~\ref{sec:black_scholes_model} as the Black--Scholes volatility that reproduces a given target option value. This section focuses on the numerical inversion used to compute it for market quotes or model-generated prices. In the workflow of this thesis, this inversion is used to build the implied-volatility labels for the supervised ANN baseline and ANN Sobolev models. By contrast, the PINN branch is trained in price space through the Heston PDE, so IV inversion is not part of its primary loss.

Numerically, we solve the scalar nonlinear equation
\begin{equation}
    F(\sigma):=V_{BS}^{\mathrm{put}}(S_0,K,r,\tau,\sigma)-V_{\text{target}}=0,\qquad \sigma>0.
\end{equation}
For European vanilla options, $V_{BS}$ is strictly increasing in $\sigma$ when $\tau>0$, so the root is unique whenever $V_{\text{target}}$ satisfies static no-arbitrage bounds.

The inversion routine used in this thesis is the bracketed Brent method \cite[Ch.~9]{press2007numerical}. Brent's method keeps a root bracket and combines bisection with secant and inverse-quadratic interpolation steps, falling back to bisection when the interpolated step is not reliable. This choice is robust for the one-dimensional IV root and avoids derivative-sensitivity issues in low-vega regions. As an alternative, the least-squares method of Levenberg and Marquardt \cite{levenberg1944,marquardt1963} was also tested in preliminary experiments, but it showed lower numerical stability in this workflow; therefore, it is not used in the final pipeline.

Stopping is based on standard price and parameter tolerances:
\begin{equation}
    |F(\sigma_n)|\le \varepsilon_{\text{price}}
    \quad\text{or}\quad
    |\sigma_{n+1}-\sigma_n|\le \varepsilon_{\sigma},
    \qquad n\le n_{\max}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\sigma_n$ is the volatility iterate at step $n$, $\varepsilon_{\text{price}}$ and $\varepsilon_\sigma$ are stopping tolerances, and $n_{\max}$ is the maximum number of iterations. If convergence is not reached, or if the target price violates arbitrage bounds, the point is flagged as invalid for IV reporting. In edge cases near the IV floor, the pricing stage may be recomputed with a wider COS truncation interval before retrying inversion.

The benchmark metrics used to evaluate the resulting IV, price, and Greek quantities are defined in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_error_metrics}.
